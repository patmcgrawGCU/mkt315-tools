Why Marketing Research Matters & How It Works
Research types · Secondary vs. primary data · Qualitative vs. quantitative techniques · Validity & reliability
🃏
Module 1
Key Terms — Click any card to flip
Marketing Research
↻ tap to flip
The collection, recording, and analysis of information to aid in making marketing decisions. Provides better data so organizations can make better decisions.
Toyota researching consumer EV adoption barriers before designing the bZ4X.
Exploratory Research
↻ tap to flip
Open-ended, flexible research used to gain initial understanding and discover the right questions to ask. Used at the beginning stages. Small samples — cannot draw statistically valid conclusions.
Key question answered: "What should we be asking?"
Descriptive Research
↻ tap to flip
Provides a detailed understanding of the characteristics of a population or phenomenon. Answers WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN — but NOT WHY. Results can be numerical and observational.
Asking drivers how far they drive daily, or ranking vehicle features on a scale.
Causal Research
↻ tap to flip
Establishes cause-and-effect between variables by manipulating one variable while holding all others constant. Highly structured. Also called experimental research or A/B testing.
Toyota testing two email offers to see which generates more website clicks — only one variable differs.
Secondary Data
↻ tap to flip
Data already collected, processed, and published by the firm or another organization. Reviewed FIRST in any research process. Less expensive and faster than primary data. Sources: industry reports, government databases, academic studies.
Reading Automotive News industry reports before commissioning a consumer survey.
Primary Data
↻ tap to flip
Original information collected firsthand for a specific research purpose. Fills gaps secondary data cannot answer. Proprietary — gives the company a competitive advantage. More expensive and time-consuming.
Toyota conducting focus groups with hybrid owners to understand EV purchase hesitations.
Qualitative Research
↻ tap to flip
Open-ended research to understand consumer reasoning, opinions, and behavior. Small samples — cannot be generalized statistically. Data type: words, themes, insights. Methods: focus groups, interviews, observational research.
Often the STARTING point — identifies what to measure before a large survey is designed.
Quantitative Research
↻ tap to flip
Structured research generating numerical data for statistical conclusions. Large samples required. Data type: numbers, percentages, statistics. Methods: surveys, behavioral research, A/B testing, analytics. Presented to leadership for major decisions.
Surveying 1,000 consumers using a Likert scale to measure brand loyalty.
Likert Scale
↻ tap to flip
A research instrument where respondents rate their agreement with a statement on a 1–5 scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Converts attitudes to numbers for statistical comparison.
"The new packaging makes the product look more premium." [1–2–3–4–5]
Validity
↻ tap to flip
The research actually measures what it claims to measure. A valid study asks questions that directly address the research question without mixing in unrelated variables.
Asking "Do you like this car?" when you want to measure GPS feature preference is NOT valid.
Reliability
↻ tap to flip
Research results can be consistently reproduced when the study is repeated under the same conditions. A reliable study produces stable results across different samples and time points.
Surveying two different groups of 500 drivers with the same questions and getting similar results.
Data Collection
↻ tap to flip
The gathering of information in a structured way to answer marketing research questions. Channels include: mail, telephone, in-person, online, mobile devices, and social media. Each channel has different tradeoffs in cost, speed, and data quality.
Online surveys are now preferred — low cost, fast deployment, instant analysis.

Scenario 01
A regional bakery chain is considering adding a new line of gluten-free products. Before spending $40,000 on product development, the owner asks the marketing team what they should do first. Which recommendation is MOST aligned with sound research practice?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
Secondary data is always reviewed FIRST. The bakery's own sales history and published industry reports on the gluten-free market are faster and cheaper than primary research — and they may fully answer whether there's a real opportunity before spending anything on development.
Scenario 02
A shoe brand runs a TV ad for two weeks in City A and runs no ad in City B (same demographics). They then measure purchase rates in both cities. What type of research is this, and why?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
This is causal (experimental) research. The ad is the independent variable. The purchase rate is the dependent variable. All other conditions (demographics, market size, time period) are held constant. That isolation of one variable is what defines causal research — it allows you to attribute the change in outcome to the change in the variable.
Scenario 03
A startup food brand wants to understand whether its new plant-based snack bar will succeed in the market. The CEO says they should survey 500 grocery shoppers immediately. A marketing consultant pushes back and says that's not the right first move. What should they do FIRST, and why?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
Secondary data is always reviewed FIRST. Before spending money on surveys, focus groups, or retail tests, the startup should check what already exists: industry reports on plant-based snack growth (IBISWorld, Mintel), government nutrition trend data, competitor sales figures, and any internal data on similar products they've sold before. This secondary research may fully answer the question — or at minimum, it will identify exactly what primary research is still needed. The principle: never commission primary research until you've exhausted secondary data.
Scenario 04
A marketing manager reviews her company's study: "92% of customers say they would recommend us to a friend." Three months later, a repeat study with a different sample yields 91%. She concludes the data is trustworthy. What quality of research is she observing?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
Reliability = consistent results when the study is repeated. The two results (92% and 91%) are nearly identical despite using different respondents — that consistency is the definition of reliability. Validity would apply if she were assessing whether "would recommend" is the right question to measure customer satisfaction (does the question actually measure what we think it measures?).
Scenario 05
An auto insurance company wants to understand WHY Gen Z drivers are more likely to let their policies lapse. Which approach is most appropriate as a FIRST step?
✓ Correct! Option A is right.
The question asks about WHY — that's a qualitative, exploratory problem. Before you can survey 5,000 people or A/B test reminders, you need to understand the underlying reasons. In-depth interviews with recent lapses give you that depth of understanding. The findings from those interviews would inform the right quantitative survey questions later. Note: D (secondary data) would actually be step zero — check what's already published — but assuming that's been done, the next step is exploratory qualitative.

Quick Check — True or False?
1. Secondary data is always less reliable than primary data and should only be used when primary research is unavailable.
FALSE
Secondary data is not inferior — it's reviewed FIRST because it's faster and cheaper. Well-sourced secondary data (government reports, industry databases, peer-reviewed studies) is often highly reliable. The "limitation" of secondary data is not reliability — it's specificity. It may not answer your exact research question, which is when primary data is needed.
2. A focus group with 8 participants is an example of qualitative exploratory research.
TRUE
A focus group with 8 participants is a classic qualitative research method. Small group, open-ended discussion, unstructured exploration of consumer thoughts — this is exploratory by nature. It uncovers themes and questions rather than producing statistically valid numbers.
3. Causal research can establish a cause-and-effect relationship even if multiple variables change at the same time.
FALSE
The defining rule of causal research is that only ONE variable changes at a time. If you change two things simultaneously, you cannot determine which change caused the effect. This is the fundamental discipline of experimental design — isolate the independent variable to establish causation. If you change a website button's color AND its copy at the same time, you don't know which change drove the conversion difference.
4. A researcher conducting one-on-one interviews with 12 consumers to understand WHY they stopped using a subscription service is using the same type of research as a researcher who surveys 2,000 consumers on a 1–5 rating scale.
FALSE
These are two fundamentally different research approaches. The 12-consumer interviews are qualitative research — small sample, open-ended, exploratory, focused on understanding reasoning and behavior. The 2,000-consumer rating-scale survey is quantitative research — large sample, structured, produces numerical data that can be statistically analyzed. The distinction is not just sample size: it's the type of data collected (words/insights vs. numbers/statistics) and the purpose (discover/understand vs. measure/confirm).
5. A survey with 5,000 structured questions answered by a large sample is an example of exploratory research.
FALSE
A large-sample structured survey is quantitative descriptive research — not exploratory. Exploratory research uses small samples, open-ended methods (focus groups, interviews), and flexible structures. The moment you have structured questions and a large sample producing numerical data, you've crossed into quantitative territory. Descriptive if it characterizes the population; causal if it's testing a variable manipulation.

Research process steps · PII & data privacy · Cambridge Analytica · Insights Association Code · Toyota & Coca-Cola cases
🃏
Module 1
Key Terms — Click any card to flip
The 5-Step Research Process
↻ tap to flip
1. Identify the Problem → 2. Develop the Research Plan → 3. Conduct Research → 4. Analyze Data & Report Findings → 5. Take Action. The process is CYCLICAL — it restarts as markets change.
Step 1 is the most important — garbage in, garbage out.
Personally Identifiable Information (PII)
↻ tap to flip
Any data that can identify a specific individual, either alone or combined with other information. Examples: name, email, phone, browsing history, location patterns, purchase records. Protected by GDPR, CCPA, and other privacy laws.
Cambridge Analytica harvested PII from 87 million Facebook users without consent via a quiz app.
A/B Testing
↻ tap to flip
An experimental research method where two versions (A and B) are tested against each other by changing ONE variable while holding all other conditions constant. The result establishes which version produces the better outcome.
Toyota testing whether an extended warranty offer OR a tire pricing offer generates more email opens.
Behavioral Research
↻ tap to flip
Seeks to understand consumer buying patterns through consumption data, data mining, and tracking the customer journey. Analyzes what people actually DO rather than what they say they'll do — more predictive than stated preference.
Amazon's recommendation engine analyzes purchase history and browsing behavior to predict next purchases.
Marketing Analytics
↻ tap to flip
Code placed on website backends and tracking tools that reveal visitor origins, page behavior, conversion rates, and online habits. Provides continuous data on digital marketing performance. Examples: Google Analytics, Adobe Analytics.
Analytics reveals that 70% of visitors leave the checkout page without buying — prompting redesign.
Neuromarketing / Physiological Research
↻ tap to flip
Applies neuroscience to marketing by measuring unconscious physiological responses to marketing stimuli. Methods include EEG (brain activity), eye tracking, and fMRI. Captures reactions consumers cannot articulate in surveys.
Eye tracking shows consumers look at a person's face on a webpage before reading the headline — redesign accordingly.
Focus Group
↻ tap to flip
A qualitative research method assembling 6–10 demographically diverse people for a guided discussion. Used in exploratory research to understand consumer reasoning, not to generate statistics. Moderator skill significantly impacts quality.
Coca-Cola used global focus groups across markets to understand what "novelty" means to Gen Z before designing Creations.
Insights Association Code of Standards
↻ tap to flip
Professional ethics code for marketing researchers requiring: (1) Respect subjects' rights, (2) Transparent consent for data collection, (3) Integrity in all relationships, (4) Compliance with applicable laws. Also prohibits revealing subject identity without consent.
Disguising data mining as an academic quiz — as Cambridge Analytica did — violates all four principles.
Research Validity & Problem Definition
↻ tap to flip
The MOST critical step in research is Step 1: Identify the Problem. A vague problem produces useless research. A strong problem statement is specific, decision-oriented, and identifies the exact information needed. Weak: "We need more data." Strong: "Which segment will pay a premium for Feature X, and what is their price ceiling?"

0 / 5 answered
Scenario 01
A streaming service notices that subscribers over 45 are canceling at twice the rate of younger subscribers. Their VP of Marketing says: "We need to run a massive survey right away." A senior researcher pushes back. What step has the VP skipped?
✓ Correct! Option A is right.
The VP has skipped Step 1 — defining the problem. "Subscribers over 45 are canceling more" is an observation, not a research problem. The research question needs to specify: Why are they canceling? Is it content? Price? Interface? Competitor? Without a clear problem definition, a massive survey will generate a lot of data and no useful insight. "Garbage in, garbage out" — the most important step is always the first one.
Scenario 02
A consumer app company creates a fun personality quiz on their website. The quiz is marketed as a "fun way to learn about yourself." In reality, it collects detailed data on users' values, interests, and social networks, which the company then sells to third-party advertisers. Which ethical principle does this MOST directly violate?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
This mirrors the Cambridge Analytica scandal precisely. Users were deceived about the purpose of the quiz (entertainment vs. data harvesting) and were not told their data would be sold. This violates the Insights Association's principle of transparency and informed consent — and in many jurisdictions, it's also illegal under GDPR and CCPA. The data may also be sold in ways that reveal individual identities, violating the additional requirement that research data not be used to identify subjects without consent.
Scenario 03
Toyota's EV research found that range anxiety was the #1 barrier to adoption. They designed the bZ4X with 252 miles of range and offered free charging for year one. Which step in the research process does the free charging offer BEST exemplify?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
Step 5 is "Take Action" — where research findings are translated into specific marketing decisions. The finding (range anxiety and charging uncertainty are barriers) led directly to the decision (offer free charging for year one to remove those specific barriers). This is exactly what Step 5 looks like: a research finding maps to a specific product or marketing decision. The bZ4X case is a model of research-driven decision-making where you can trace every major feature directly back to a Step 4 finding.
Scenario 04
Coca-Cola launched limited-edition Creations flavors that were available for a short time only. Cherry Vanilla and Orange Vanilla eventually became permanent products. From a research perspective, what was brilliant about this strategy?
✓ Correct! Option D is right.
This is one of the most elegant examples of embedding research into a product strategy. By making Creations limited-edition, Coca-Cola ran a real-world test of each flavor — revealed preference (what people actually buy) rather than stated preference (what people say they'll buy in a survey). The flavors that sold well became permanent; the ones that didn't were retired. The sales data is the research. This eliminates the survey gap: people can intend to buy a flavor and never actually do it — but they can't lie with a purchase.
Scenario 05
A researcher presents findings to a client showing that 78% of users prefer the competitor's mobile app. The client says, "This can't be right — let's leave this finding out of the report." A researcher who agrees to omit this finding is violating which principle?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
Selectively reporting research to omit unfavorable findings is a fundamental integrity violation. Research exists to inform better decisions — including uncomfortable ones. A client who doesn't know their competitor's app is preferred by 78% of users will continue making decisions based on false confidence. The Insights Association Code specifically requires "integrity, professionalism, and transparency in all relationships and practices." Omitting a finding to please a client is the opposite of that — and it ultimately harms the client whose decisions will be based on incomplete information.

Quick Check — True or False?
1. Step 1 of the research process (Identify the Problem) is the most critical step because every subsequent step depends on getting it right.
TRUE
If the research problem is poorly defined, every dollar spent on the remaining four steps is potentially wasted. Research conducted to answer the wrong question produces data that can't drive good decisions — "garbage in, garbage out." The problem statement must be specific, decision-oriented, and identify exactly what information is needed. Vague problems produce vague results.
2. The marketing research process ends at Step 5 (Take Action) — once decisions are made, the process is complete.
FALSE
The 5-step process is CYCLICAL — it doesn't end at Step 5. After decisions are implemented, the marketplace continues to change: competitors respond, consumer preferences shift, economic conditions evolve. This creates new questions that restart the research process. Toyota doesn't do EV research once — it researches continuously as the market changes. Excellent marketing organizations have ongoing research capabilities, not just occasional research projects.
3. Under the Insights Association Code, researchers are required to ensure that data collected for research is not used to reveal the identity of a research subject without their consent.
TRUE
This is a specific requirement in the Insights Association Code: "Ensure that data obtained for purposes of research are not used to reveal the identity of the research subject without their consent." This goes beyond simply not sharing names — it means anonymization practices must ensure that combinations of data points cannot be used to identify individuals. This is especially relevant in the age of data re-identification, where even "anonymous" data can sometimes be traced back to individuals.
4. Behavioral research (tracking what consumers actually do) is generally more predictive of future behavior than stated preference surveys (asking what consumers say they'll do).
TRUE
Past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior. There is a well-documented gap between what people SAY they'll do and what they actually DO — the "intention-action gap." Coca-Cola's Creations strategy exploited this by using real purchase data rather than survey data to decide which flavors to keep permanently. A survey respondent might say they'd buy a Cotton Candy Coke — but actual purchase behavior tells the real story.
5. Cambridge Analytica violated data ethics by collecting data from approximately 305,000 people who actively consented to participating in a quiz.
FALSE — but requires nuance.
The 305,000 who took the quiz did consent to having THEIR OWN data collected. The violation was that Facebook's API at the time allowed the app to also access the data of those users' Facebook FRIENDS — approximately 87 million people who had never heard of the quiz and never consented. Framing the violation as affecting the 305,000 active participants understates the harm by a factor of nearly 300x.

Objectives, Strategies, Tactics & Situation Analysis
O-S-T hierarchy · Strategic planning process · SWOT analysis · BCG Matrix · Ansoff Matrix · Environmental scan
🃏
Module 1
Key Terms — Click any card to flip
Objective
↻ tap to flip
WHAT you want to achieve. A specific, measurable outcome aligned with organizational mission. Long-term — typically measured in years. Must have a TARGET (what) and TIME LIMIT (when).
Mastercard: "Drive greater share of spend among consumers — +25% within 2 years."
Strategy
↻ tap to flip
HOW you plan to achieve the objective. Directional approach — big ideas, not specific actions. Medium-term, often measured in months. Must be tested: "Does this strategy actually drive toward the objective?"
Mastercard: Extend card use from luxury purchases (travel, dining) to everyday spending (grocery, gas).
Tactic
↻ tap to flip
A specific ACTION taken to implement the strategy. Short-term, day-to-day or week-to-week. Detailed and budgeted. Each tactic must tie back to a specific strategy, which in turn serves the objective.
Mastercard: Launch the "Priceless" TV campaign. Sign up grocery stores to accept Mastercard. Partner with MLB.
Situation Analysis
↻ tap to flip
A thorough, honest assessment of where an organization stands relative to its products, competitors, and market direction. Examines four perspectives: Environment, Competitors, Company, and Consumers. Diagnose BEFORE prescribing strategy.
Netflix's situation analysis while still a DVD company revealed: streaming is an opportunity AND physical media is a fatal weakness.
Environmental Scan
↻ tap to flip
Analysis of external forces outside any company's control that affect the entire market. STEC framework: Social (demographics, culture, norms), Technology (disruption, purchase behavior), Economic (GDP, inflation, consumer income), Competitive (market structure, competitor activity).
SWOT Analysis
↻ tap to flip
Strategic tool analyzing Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. S & W = INTERNAL (within company control). O & T = EXTERNAL (outside company control). Value is proportional to honesty — wishful thinking produces false confidence.
Netflix DVD-era: Strength = customer loyalty; Weakness = physical media dependence; Opportunity = streaming growth; Threat = digital competitors.
Ansoff Matrix
↻ tap to flip
Strategic tool assessing four growth options by risk level: Market Penetration (existing product + existing market — LOWEST risk), Product Development (new product + existing market), Market Development (existing product + new market), Diversification (new product + new market — HIGHEST risk).
Risk increases every time you cross into a new market or new product category.
BCG Growth-Share Matrix
↻ tap to flip
Portfolio tool classifying products/businesses by market growth rate and market share: Stars (high growth, high share — invest), Cash Cows (low growth, high share — harvest), Question Marks (high growth, low share — decide), Dogs/Pets (low growth, low share — divest).
Use Cash Cow profits to fund Stars; evaluate Question Marks; exit Dogs.
KPIs (Key Performance Indicators)
↻ tap to flip
Measurement tools that tell marketers when they have achieved key points of success AND allow them to recognize areas that are off-track in time to correct them. Essential for marketing plan control. Dashboard of a strategy.
If objective is +5% market share in 12 months, KPIs might include monthly new customer acquisition, competitive win rate, and brand awareness tracking.
Implementation Plan
↻ tap to flip
The roadmap of activities, budgets, timelines, and milestones needed to execute the marketing plan. Translates strategy into operational reality — who does what, when, and with what resources. Makes objectives achievable.
Competitive Market Structures
↻ tap to flip
Four structures: Pure Competition (many sellers, identical products — distribution differentiates), Monopolistic Competition (many sellers, substitutable — promotions differentiate), Oligopoly (few sellers, most sales — quality/service differentiates), Monopoly (one seller, regulated — marketing minimal).

Scenario 01
A marketing director writes: "Our strategy for Q3 is to post three times per week on Instagram and run two email campaigns per month." A junior team member responds that this isn't a strategy. Who is correct, and why?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
Posting three times per week and running two email campaigns are TACTICS — specific, executable, day-to-day actions. A strategy would say something like: "Build an authentic community among Gen Z fitness enthusiasts through social platforms they trust, using peer content and challenge formats that drive organic sharing." THAT directional approach is a strategy. The specific tactics (what platform, how often, what content format) would flow from that strategy. The most common mistake is writing tactics when asked for strategies.
Scenario 02
A running shoe brand with strong brand loyalty and premium positioning decides to launch a line of tennis racquets. Using the Ansoff Matrix, where does this decision sit — and what does that mean for risk?
✓ Correct! Option D is right.
Diversification occurs when a company pursues BOTH a new product AND a new market simultaneously — the highest-risk Ansoff quadrant. The shoe brand has no expertise in making racquets (new product) and no existing relationship with tennis players (new market). Compare this to Product Development (making running apparel for existing runners — new product, existing market) which would be lower risk because the customer relationship is already established. Risk increases every time you cross a new axis in the Ansoff Matrix.
Scenario 03
A marketing team presents their SWOT with these entries: Strengths — "We have great employees and a good culture." Weaknesses — "Sometimes we could communicate better internally." A consultant calls this SWOT "useless." Why?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
"We have great employees" is not a competitive strength — it's an aspiration that virtually every company claims. A real strength is something specific and advantageous vs. competitors: "Our customer retention rate (94%) exceeds the industry average (70%)." Similarly, "we could communicate better" softens a real weakness into something non-actionable. A SWOT's value is directly proportional to its honesty. Vague entries produce vague strategy. The consultant's critique is about specificity and evidence — not quantity or structure.
Scenario 04
Netflix's decision to invest in streaming in 2007 while still operating a successful DVD-by-mail business is best described as which Ansoff Matrix strategy — and what drove it?
✓ Note: This is a genuinely debatable classification.
This question has legitimate arguments for both B (Market Development — same content, new delivery channel/market) and D (Product Development — new delivery method for existing customers). The strongest argument is D: Netflix's existing subscribers were the target, and streaming was a new product/service offering for them. The key SWOT insight that drove the decision: Opportunity (growing internet speeds, digital lifestyle) + Weakness (physical media dependence) = the streaming strategy was both necessary and possible. Their honest SWOT forced the move.
Scenario 05
A large consumer electronics company has four products: (1) Wireless earbuds — growing 35% per year, market leader. (2) Desktop computers — declining market, they still lead. (3) Smart home hub — growing market, they have 8% share. (4) Fax machines — declining market, minimal share. Using the BCG Matrix, correctly classify all four.
✓ Correct! Option A is right.
BCG Matrix: High growth + high share = Star (Earbuds: growing 35%, market leader → invest). Low growth + high share = Cash Cow (Desktops: declining market, still leading → harvest profits, fund Stars). High growth + low share = Question Mark (Smart Home Hub: growing market, only 8% share → decide: invest to win, or exit?). Low growth + low share = Dog (Fax: declining market, minimal share → divest or let die). Strategic implication: Use Desktop profits (Cash Cow) to fund Earbuds growth (Star) and potentially the Smart Home Hub (Question Mark).

Quick Check — True or False?
1. Objectives, strategies, and tactics are essentially different words for the same concept — they all refer to what a marketing team plans to do.
FALSE
These are distinct concepts operating at different time horizons and levels of specificity. Objectives = WHAT (measurable long-term outcomes, measured in years). Strategies = HOW (directional approaches, measured in months). Tactics = specific actions (day-to-day/week-to-week execution). The analogy: Objective = your destination city. Strategy = the route you take. Tactics = specific turns, speed, and stops along the way. Confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in marketing planning.
2. In a SWOT analysis, regulatory changes that restrict how a company can advertise would be classified as a Threat.
TRUE
Regulatory changes are external factors outside the company's control. If they negatively affect the company's operations or competitive position — such as restricting advertising methods the company relies on — they classify as Threats. If the same regulatory change restricted competitors more than your company (perhaps you already use compliant methods), the same change could be an Opportunity. The classification depends on the impact on YOUR company's position.
3. The BCG Matrix would advise investing heavily in "Dog" products because they represent untapped growth potential.
FALSE
Dogs (low growth, low market share) represent the opposite of untapped potential — they're in declining markets where the company is also a weak competitor. The BCG Matrix recommendation for Dogs is to DIVEST (sell or shut down) or harvest whatever remaining value exists, then stop investing. The resources freed from Dogs should be redirected to Stars and selective Question Marks. Investing heavily in Dogs is described as "throwing good money after bad."
4. Situation analysis should come BEFORE setting marketing objectives — you must diagnose the situation before prescribing a strategy.
TRUE
This is the core principle of the strategic planning sequence: diagnose before prescribing. A doctor doesn't prescribe medication before examining the patient. A marketer shouldn't set objectives before understanding where the organization currently stands relative to the market, competitors, consumers, and internal capabilities. The situation analysis produces the evidence that makes objectives realistic and strategies grounded in actual conditions.
5. Moving from Market Penetration to Diversification in the Ansoff Matrix increases risk because both the product and the market are new territory.
TRUE
Market Penetration = existing product + existing market = lowest risk (you know the product and the customer). Diversification = new product + new market = highest risk (you know neither the product nor the customer). Risk increases every time you cross into unfamiliar territory. Market Development and Product Development are intermediate risks — you're unfamiliar with one dimension (either the market or the product) but have existing knowledge in the other.

Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning & the Marketing Mix
4 segmentation types · Target market criteria · Buyer personas · Differentiation · Positioning · SMART goals · 4Ps + 7Ps
🃏
Module 1
Key Terms — Click any card to flip
Market Segmentation
↻ tap to flip
Dividing the entire available market into groups of consumers with similar characteristics. Principle: narrowing your focus increases the power of your message. "The more you focus, the stronger your brand becomes — because you stand for something." (Al Ries)
Geographic Segmentation
↻ tap to flip
Narrows the market by WHERE consumers are — country, region, city, zip code, or climate. Broadest form of segmentation.
A restaurant targets customers within a 5-mile radius. A solar panel company focuses on high-sun states.
Demographic Segmentation
↻ tap to flip
Narrows by WHO consumers are — age, gender, income, ethnicity, education, occupation, marital status, household size, birth era (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z). Most commonly used because demographic data is abundant.
Restaurant targets families with children, household income $65K+.
Psychographic Segmentation
↻ tap to flip
Narrows by HOW consumers think — values, beliefs, personality, social status, activities, interests, opinions, political leanings. Goes beyond who someone IS to how they see themselves and the world.
Restaurant campaign emphasizes clean, fun, family environment for health-conscious parents — same demographic, different psychographic.
Behavioral Segmentation
↻ tap to flip
Narrows by WHAT consumers DO — purchase occasions, loyalty status, usage rate, benefits sought, buyer-readiness stage. The most predictive form of segmentation: past behavior predicts future behavior. 80/20 rule: 80% of sales from 20% of customers.
Loyalty program data reveals which customers visit most often and what they order.
Target Market
↻ tap to flip
The specific segment of the population selected by the company to direct its marketing efforts toward. Must be: most likely to buy, large enough for revenue goals, reachable through channels, NOT fiercely loyal to a competitor, profitable to serve.
Mastercard targeted "Good Revolvers" — responsible everyday credit users ignored by Amex and Visa.
Buyer Persona
↻ tap to flip
A constructed representation of the ideal buyer with all traits of the target segment — demographics, lifestyle, media habits, consumption patterns, professional role, and emotional drivers. Used to filter every marketing decision: "Would this resonate with [Persona Name]?"
"Sarah, 34, marketing manager, $95K HHI, two kids, drives a Honda CRV, reads Fast Company, shops at Target."
Differentiation
↻ tap to flip
Creating points of difference in the consumer's mind that allow a company to stand out from competitors. Must be based on ADDED VALUE (above expectations), not expected value (good service is expected — not a differentiator). Three paths: price, customer service, brand image.
Dawn differentiates by cleaning wildlife harmed by oil spills — emotional brand integrity above product function.
Positioning
↻ tap to flip
Owning a unique and compelling spot in the consumer's mind different from any competitor. "Positioning is not what you do to a product. It's what you do to the MIND of the prospect." (Ries & Trout). Happens whether you plan it or not — position deliberately before someone else does.
Mastercard positioned itself as the card for meaningful everyday moments — territory Amex and Visa didn't own.
Repositioning
↻ tap to flip
Changing the place a product or brand occupies in the consumer's mind relative to competitors. Needed when: position is unfavorable, brand wants to expand into new areas, or mission has significantly changed. Requires time, consistency, and investment.
Apple repositioned from "computer company for creative professionals" to "lifestyle technology brand for everyone" via the iPod.
Perceptual Map
↻ tap to flip
A visualization tool using two axes to graph the position of a brand compared to competitors based on consumer perceptions. Reveals: who occupies competing positions, where market gaps exist, and where new positioning opportunities are available.
X-axis = Price (Low→High), Y-axis = Quality (Low→High). An empty High Quality / Low Price quadrant signals an opportunity.
SMART Goals
↻ tap to flip
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-Bound. Created by George Doran (1981). Transforms ambitious but vague intentions into concrete, accountable plans with clear milestones and measurement criteria.
Weak: "Grow revenue." SMART: "Increase monthly subscription revenue by 20% within 18 months by expanding into the small business segment."
The 4 Ps (Marketing Mix)
↻ tap to flip
Product (what you sell — tangible or intangible, features, packaging), Price (what customers pay — must account for competitiveness, perceived value, cost, margin), Place (how customers access it — distribution channels, logistics), Promotion (how consumers learn about it — advertising, PR, events, digital).
All four Ps must align with each other AND with the target market and positioning strategy.
The Extended 7 Ps (Services)
↻ tap to flip
Services add three Ps to the classic four: People (front-line employees ARE the service experience), Process (systems ensuring consistent experience across every interaction and location), Physical Evidence (the tangible elements consumers see/touch/feel that signal quality).
Starbucks: People (trained baristas), Process (standardized drink prep), Physical Evidence (store design identical worldwide).
VALS / PRIZM / Tapestry
↻ tap to flip
Pre-built segmentation systems: VALS (8 psychographic types by motivation and resources), PRIZM/Claritas (68 lifestyle segments combining demographics, behavior, geography), Tapestry/ESRI (67 neighborhood segments). Save research time by providing ready-to-use segmentation profiles.
VALS "Strivers" buy above their means to project status. PRIZM "Young Achievers" = ideal target for tech startups.

Scenario 01
A direct-to-consumer mattress brand has identified two potential target segments: (A) adults 25–45 with back pain who prioritize sleep quality and will pay a premium, and (B) all adults who sleep on a mattress. Their marketing budget is $200,000. A strategist argues strongly for Segment A. What is the PRIMARY strategic reason to choose the narrower segment?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
This is the resource allocation argument for focus. Segment B ("all adults who sleep") contains every competitor's target — the messaging must be generic to reach everyone, and $200K spread across that universe produces minimal impact. Segment A allows the brand to speak directly to a specific unmet need (back pain + sleep quality), use targeted media channels (health publications, orthopedic forums, sleep-specialist influencers), and build a brand that means something specific to a group likely to buy, pay a premium, and refer others. Focus doesn't limit revenue — it concentrates the resources needed to earn it.
Scenario 02
Two dish soap brands, Dawn and Mrs. Meyer's, are both more expensive than the generic store brand yet maintain strong customer loyalty. What type of differentiation does each primarily use, and what does this illustrate about differentiation strategy?
✓ Correct! Option D is right.
Dawn's differentiation is emotional brand integrity — using the same formula to clean wildlife contaminated by oil spills creates a values association that transcends the functional product. Mrs. Meyer's plant-derived ingredients and recyclable packaging appeal to environmentally conscious consumers. Neither claim is purely about superior cleaning power — they're about what the brand stands for. This illustrates a crucial differentiation principle: you don't need a superior product to command a premium — you need a superior story that connects to what your target audience values.
Scenario 03
A fitness app brand writes this as their positioning statement: "For health-conscious consumers, we offer a high-quality, convenient workout solution at a fair price." A marketing professor circles this and writes "NOT a positioning statement — try again." Why?
✓ Correct! Option B is right.
"Quality, convenient, and fair price" are EXPECTED values — they're the minimum consumers expect from any product worth considering. A positioning statement must identify ADDED value — what this brand delivers above and beyond the baseline that no competitor can legitimately claim. Ries and Trout define positioning as "what you do to the mind of the prospect" — and this statement doesn't give any consumer a reason to mentally place this brand in a distinct, memorable position. The fix: identify the one thing this app does that competitors don't, and build the statement around that.
Scenario 04
A national hotel chain wants to win over business travelers. Their marketing plan includes: a comfortable lobby, free Wi-Fi, and friendly staff. A branding consultant says these 4Ps decisions are strategically weak. Why?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
"Comfortable lobby, free Wi-Fi, friendly staff" describe what EVERY business hotel offers. These are expected values — they're the price of admission. Business travelers are not going to choose one hotel over another because it has Wi-Fi. They choose based on differentiators: guaranteed late check-in, 24-hour room service, proximity to airports and convention centers, elite loyalty program, or specific workspace design. The 4Ps must be connected to what the target market (business travelers) actually needs that competitors don't deliver. That connection is what makes them strategically strong, not just competent.
Scenario 05
A marketing director sets this goal for her team: "We want to significantly increase our social media following in the next year." A mentor says this fails the SMART test. Which letter(s) of SMART does it fail, and what would a SMART version look like?
✓ Correct! Option C is right.
"Significantly increase" fails S (what platform? what type of followers? what's the starting point?) and M (significantly by how much? there's no number to track against). Without a baseline and a specific target, there's no way to know what "achieved" looks like or to set meaningful milestones. The SMART version (12,400 → 25,000 by Dec 31 via Reels + micro-influencers) passes all five tests: Specific (Instagram, follower count, strategy type), Measurable (exact numbers), Achievable (roughly 2x growth is ambitious but realistic with the right tactics), Relevant (social growth supports brand awareness), and Time-Bound (December 31).

Quick Check — True or False?
1. Behavioral segmentation is generally the most PREDICTIVE form of segmentation because past behavior is the strongest indicator of future behavior.
TRUE
Behavioral segmentation tracks what people actually DO — purchase history, loyalty status, usage rate, benefits sought. Actual behavior is far more predictive than demographics (who someone is) or psychographics (how they think), because it captures what people have already demonstrated they will do. The 80/20 rule (80% of sales from 20% of customers) is a behavioral insight — and loyalty programs exist specifically to identify and nurture those high-value behavioral segments.
2. The 7Ps of marketing (adding People, Process, and Physical Evidence) are relevant for ALL businesses — not just service companies.
FALSE — with nuance.
The 7Ps extension is specifically designed for SERVICE businesses where the experience IS the product. People, Process, and Physical Evidence matter most when the quality of the service delivery is what the customer is paying for and evaluating. For pure physical product companies (like a manufacturer selling widgets through distributors), the classic 4Ps are typically sufficient. The 7Ps become essential when there's significant human interaction in the delivery of value — hotels, restaurants, healthcare, consulting, education.
3. A perceptual map shows how consumers actually perceive brands, which may differ significantly from how the brands wish to be perceived.
TRUE
A perceptual map is built on consumer research — it shows how consumers position brands in their minds, not how companies position themselves in their marketing materials. This distinction matters: a company may believe it occupies a "high-quality, affordable" position, but if consumers perceive it as low-quality and overpriced, the map will show that reality. This is why repositioning is so challenging — you're not changing the product, you're changing beliefs that consumers have already formed.
4. VALS, PRIZM, and Tapestry are primarily geographic segmentation tools — they segment consumers based on where they live.
FALSE
While PRIZM and Tapestry incorporate geographic data (zip codes, neighborhoods), all three tools are multi-dimensional — they combine geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral data into unified consumer profiles. VALS is primarily psychographic (values, attitudes, lifestyles). PRIZM and Tapestry use geography as an organizing structure but classify consumers by lifestyle, behavior, and socioeconomic characteristics that go well beyond simply where someone lives. Their power is precisely in combining multiple segmentation dimensions into a single actionable profile.
5. If a company doesn't deliberately position its brand, the brand will have no position in consumers' minds — it simply remains neutral.
FALSE
Brands are positioned whether or not the company does it deliberately. If a company doesn't control its positioning, competitors, the media, social networks, and consumers will define it instead — often unfavorably. A brand without deliberate positioning might be "the cheap option," "the confusing brand," or "the one that's not as good as [Competitor X]." Ries and Trout argue that this is why positioning must be proactive: "If you don't position yourself, your competition will position you." Deliberate positioning is not optional — it's what protects the brand from being defined by default.
